## Core Philosophy: The Power of Going Small

The fundamental premise of _The ONE Thing_ is simple yet transformative: **extraordinary results are directly determined by how narrow you can make your focus**.

When you want the absolute best chance to succeed at anything, your approach should always be to **go small**. "Going small" means ignoring all the things you _could_ do and focusing strictly on what you _should_ do. It comes down to recognizing that not all tasks matter equally and identifying the single most impactful lever for your goal.

> "There can only be one most important thing. Many things may be important, but only one can be the most important."  
> — _Ross Garber_

### Key Principles

- **The Secret of Focus:** Success requires narrowing down choices until you find the single leverage point that makes everything else easier or unnecessary.
- **Organizational Focus:** If a company or project does not clearly know what its ONE Thing is, then its primary job becomes finding out.
- **The Value of Mentorship:** No one succeeds in isolation. Behind every successful person, there is usually one key figure who influenced, trained, or guided them.

## Lie #1: Everything Matters Equally

The first major obstacle to extraordinary success is believing that everything on your plate carries equal importance. When everything feels urgent and important, we default to busyness—active and working hard, yet getting no closer to meaningful success.

> "It’s not enough to be busy, so are the ants. The question is, what are we busy about?"  
> — _Henry David Thoreau_

### From To-Do Lists to Success Lists

Most people operate from daily **To-Do Lists**. However, traditional to-do lists are inherently flawed:

- **They lack intent:** They are simply lists of whatever popped into your head first.
- **They act as survival lists:** They help you get through the day, but they don't turn today into a stepping-stone for long-term growth.
- **They pull you in all directions:** Long to-do lists scatter your focus.

Achievers work differently. They don't fall into the "check off" trap—checking off a hundred low-value items to end the day with a clean desk. Instead, they convert their To-Do List into a **Success List** by applying priority.

| To-Do List                   | Success List                     |
| :--------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| Long and disorganized        | Short and highly focused         |
| Filled with "Could do" items | Built around "Should do" items   |
| Pulls you in every direction | Aims you in a specific direction |

### Taking Pareto to the Extreme (Extreme Pareto)

The Pareto Principle (the 80/20 rule) states that 80% of your results come from 20% of your efforts. But Gary Keller points out that Pareto doesn't go far enough—you need to take it to the extreme:

1. Start with your full list of tasks (e.g., 25 items).
2. Apply the 20% rule to find the vital few (5 items).
3. **Apply the 20% rule again** to find the vital few of the vital few, until you arrive at **the ONE Thing** (1 item).

### Key Takeaways for Lie #1

1. **Focus on productivity, not busyness:** Allow what matters most to drive your day.
2. **Go extreme:** Keep paring down your tasks until you find the single core activity that belongs at the top of your Success List.
3. **Master the "Not Now":** Say "later" or "never" to non-essential tasks until your primary work is finished.
4. **Avoid the "check-off" trap:** Success isn't measured by how many items you cross off, but by doing what matters most.

## Lie #2: Multitasking

Multitasking is a lie because doing two things at once effectively is a cognitive impossibility when both tasks require focus. While you can perform two mechanical actions simultaneously (e.g., walking and talking), you cannot concentrate effectively on two cognitive tasks at the same time.

> "You can do two things at once, but you can't focus effectively on two things at once."

### The Hidden Cost of Task Switching

Every time you get interrupted or switch tasks, your brain pays a hidden penalty:

1. **Switch Cost:** You lose time shutting down one task context and loading another into your working memory.
2. **Reorientation Time:** It takes time to regain deep focus after returning to the original work.

**An interrupted workflow:**

1. You begin your primary work.
2. A distraction pulls your attention away.
3. You switch back, then spend time reorienting before you can focus deeply again.

When you can stay focused, you avoid those extra switching and recovery steps.

### Why Multitasking Harms Success

- **Increased Error Rates:** Multitaskers make more mistakes than non-multitaskers.
- **Flawed Decision-Making:** Multitaskers tend to favor new, novel information over older information, even when the older information is significantly more valuable.
- **Stress and Burnout:** Constant task switching causes cognitive overload, friction, and unnecessary mental exhaustion.

### Key Takeaways for Lie #2

1. **Distraction is natural:** Everyone gets distracted; don't beat yourself up over it, but manage your environment to reduce it.
2. **Distraction undermines quality:** Trying to do too much at once leads to doing nothing well.
3. **Give undivided attention:** Identify what matters most in the current moment and dedicate 100% of your focus to it.

## Lie #3: A Disciplined Life

We often admire highly successful individuals and assume they possess an extraordinary level of personal discipline across everything they do. This is a myth.

> "It’s one of the most prevalent myths of our culture: self-discipline."  
> — _Leo Babauta_

### Success is About Doing the Right Thing, Not Everything Right

You do not need to be a completely disciplined person to be successful. In fact, you can achieve extraordinary results with far less discipline than you think.

- **Selective Discipline:** Success is not about maintaining continuous discipline across all areas of your life; it's about applying **selective discipline** to build one powerful habit at a time.
- **Habit vs. Discipline:** Discipline is hard work and requires conscious effort. A habit, once formed, runs automatically with minimal mental overhead.
- **The Perception of Discipline:** When you look at "disciplined" people, you are actually observing individuals who have trained a small set of habits into their lives. Once routine takes over, it looks like discipline, but it is simply automation.

### The 66-Day Rule: The Role of Discipline in Achievement

Research from University College London (2009) reveals that acquiring a new habit takes an average of **66 days** (ranging from 18 to 254 days depending on complexity).

> **66 days on average** to form a new habit.
> The reported range is **18 to 254 days**, depending on its complexity.

### Key Takeaways for Lie #3

1. **Don't try to be a disciplined person:** Focus on bringing _just enough discipline_ to establish one target habit.
2. **Build habits sequentially:** Focus on one habit at a time for at least **66 days** before attempting to add another.
3. **Automate success:** Once a habit is established, it works for you automatically, freeing up your mental energy to tackle the next level of growth.

## Remaining Lies to Explore

1. **Willpower Is Always on Will-Call:** Willpower is a limited energy source that depletes throughout the day.
2. **A Balanced Life:** Extraordinary success requires temporary imbalance to give proper focus to what matters most.
3. **Big Is Bad:** Thinking big is essential for achieving remarkable growth and reaching full potential.
